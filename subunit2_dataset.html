Section 2.1

Where do democracy scores come from?

In Lecture 3 you saw that “democracy” has to be defined and measured before it becomes a number. Several research teams do this every year for almost every country. News stories, governments, and researchers all use their scores. Here are three you’ll see often. Click each card to flip it.

V-Dem

Varieties of Democracy

University of Gothenburg, Sweden

Who: 4,000+ scholars and country experts

Scale: 0 to 1 (higher = more democratic)

Measures: hundreds of features of democracy. Our class dataset uses its Electoral Democracy Index.

Note: it relies on expert judgment, so V-Dem uses a statistical model to combine many experts’ ratings.

Freedom House

Freedom in the World report

Nonprofit, Washington, D.C.

Who: analysts and advisers at a U.S. nonprofit

Scale: 0–100 = Political Rights (0–40) + Civil Liberties (0–60), plus a label: Free / Partly Free / Not Free

Measures: the rights and freedoms people actually have

Note: easy to read and often quoted in the news, but one number hides a lot of detail.

Polity5

Polity Project

Center for Systemic Peace

Scale: −10 (hereditary monarchy) to +10 (full democracy), often grouped into autocracy, anocracy (mixed), and democracy

Measures: how leaders are chosen and how limited their power is

Note: no updates after 2018. Old data are still useful for history, but not for today.

These indexes differ in what they include and how they measure it, so the same country can get different scores. When a news story cites a democracy score, check which source it used.

🔬 How researchers use them. Researchers treat these scores as variables to answer big questions. For example, the review Are Democracies Really Better Governed? (Annual Review of Political Science) compares how governments perform in countries with different democracy scores.

📰 In the news: Bolivia. Freedom House’s 2026 report moved Bolivia from Partly Free (65) to Free (69). The 69 is Political Rights 30/40 plus Civil Liberties 39/60. The reason: in the 2025 elections, voters and state institutions resisted interference, and power passed peacefully to the opposition. (Freedom House: Bolivia)

Connect to Lecture 3: two sub-scores are added together to make one number.

Also in the news: V-Dem’s 2026 report called the 2026 U.S. midterms “a critical test” of election quality. Those elections were held on November 3, but they won’t appear in V-Dem’s data until its next report, which usually comes out in March. Data always lag behind events for yearly data like this. (V-Dem press release)

Section 2.2

Codebook, methodology, or research article?

You’ll run into datasets in two places. One is your own work: you might want to use a dataset for a report or presentation. The other is the news: a story might cite a figure like “Country X scored 69 out of 100.” Either way, here are the documents to check to understand where the numbers come from:

  • Codebook = the dictionary. Each variable’s name, meaning, scale, and source.
  • Methodology = the recipe. How the data were collected and combined: who rated what, which questions they answered, and how scores were added up.
  • Research article = the use. It asks a question, uses the data, and makes an argument.

Anatomy of a codebook entry

Below is the real entry for the democracy score in our class dataset. Click each highlighted part to see what it tells you.

v2x_polyarchy  ·  Interval

Electoral Democracy Index (0–1). Constructed from indicators including suffrage, clean elections, elected officials, freedom of association, and freedom of expression. Higher = more democratic. Source: V-Dem Codebook v15, Section 2.1.1.

Quick check: which document?

Statement 1

“Lists each variable’s name, what it means, and its possible values.”

That’s the dictionary job of a codebook.

Statement 2

“Explains how experts were recruited and how their ratings were combined into one score.”

How the data were made is the recipe: the methodology.

Statement 3

“Uses democracy scores to test whether democracies have lower child mortality.”

Asking a question and testing it with the data is what a research article does.

Section 2.3

How a dataset is laid out

⚠️ About our class dataset (POLI-LIT). This is a teaching dataset. It covers 1990–2014 only, so the numbers are not current. It was built for learning in this course, not for research or other classes.

Most datasets are a big table. Four terms describe it:

  • Observation (row): one case. In POLI-LIT, one country in one year.
  • Variable (column): one thing measured about each case, like the democracy score or GDP.
  • Unit of analysis: what one row is about. In POLI-LIT, it’s the country-year.
  • Missing value (NA): no data for that cell. NA is not zero.

Another example of unit of analysis. If a spreadsheet lists every USC student once, the unit is the student. If it lists each student’s GPA every semester, the unit is the student-semester: one student in one semester, just like one country in one year.

Codebook practice

Question 1

Which variable tells you how democratic a country’s neighbors are, on average?

  1. v2x_polyarchy
  2. demregion
  3. e_regionpol_6C
  4. v2x_gender

demregion is the average democracy score of all other countries in the same region. e_regionpol_6C only tells you which region a country is in.

Question 2

Mexico’s v2smgovsmcenprc rose from 0.85 in 2012 to 1.58 in 2013. According to the codebook, what does that mean?

  1. More censorship of social media
  2. Less censorship of social media
  3. More people used social media
  4. Nothing; the number has no direction

The codebook says higher values mean less censorship. Always check the direction: for some variables a higher number means less of something.

Question 3

Mexico’s e_gdppc in 2014 is 15.737. What does that mean?

  1. About $15.74 per person
  2. About $15,737 per person
  3. 15.7% economic growth
  4. Mexico ranked 15th in the world

The codebook says to treat the units as thousands (1 = $1,000). So 15.737 × 1,000 ≈ $15,700 of economic output per person.

Section 2.5

Levels of measurement

Knowing a variable’s type is the first step in analysis, because it decides which statistics and graphs make sense.

  • Nominal: categories with no natural order.
    • Example: e_regionpol_6C. Each number stands for a region (1 = Eastern Europe and Central Asia, 2 = Latin America and the Caribbean, and so on).
    • Key point: you can count how many countries are in each region, but region 4 isn’t “more” than region 2.
  • Ordinal: categories with a meaningful order, but the gaps between them aren’t equal.
    • Example: IGO_participation_level: Low, Medium, or High.
    • Key point: you can rank countries, but the jump from Low to Medium may not equal the jump from Medium to High.
  • Interval: ordered, evenly spaced numbers, but no true zero.
    • Example: year. The gap between 2000 and 2005 is five years, but “year 0” isn’t “no time,” so “twice as many years” doesn’t make sense.
  • Ratio: ordered, evenly spaced numbers with a true zero, so ratios make sense.
    • Example: GDP per capita. Zero means no income, so $20k really is twice $10k.

Example: USC enrollment data

2024 Fall, University of South Carolina, Political Science undergraduate enrollment

Variable Example value Data type
Gender Male: 46.9% / Female: 53.1% Nominal, ratio
Enrollment status Full-time: 96.9% / Part-time: 3.1% Nominal, ratio
Class standing Freshman: 133 / Sophomore: 201 / Junior: 179 / Senior: 211 Ordinal
Enrollment count 742 Ratio

Gender and enrollment status are nominal because they sort students into categories without order. The percentages, though, are ratio data, since 0% would mean no students at all. Class standing is ordinal: the categories follow a sequence, but the “distance” between them isn’t equal. Enrollment count is ratio: 742 students is twice as many as 371. (USC enrollment data)

Section 2.6

When the average misleads

  • The mean (average) adds everything up and divides by how many there are.
  • The median is the middle value when everything is lined up from lowest to highest.

When a few values are extreme, the mean gets pulled toward them, but the median stays put. That’s why income and house prices are usually reported as medians.

Try it: who walks into the room?

Ten people are in a room. Watch what happens to the mean and the median when one very rich person walks in.

Now with real data: GDP per capita, 2014

Each bar counts how many countries fall in a $5k range. (Remember: this is our teaching dataset, so these are 2014 numbers.) Most countries sit on the left, and a few very rich countries stretch far to the right. Data shaped like this are called skewed.

Key idea. When data are skewed, the mean is pulled toward the extremes. The median gives a better sense of a “typical” case. So when you see an “average,” ask: mean or median?

Quick check

A town’s average (mean) income is $95k, but its median income is $42k. What’s most likely going on?

  1. A few very high incomes pull the average up
  2. Most people earn about $95k
  3. The data must contain an error
  4. Half the town earns more than $95k

A mean far above the median is the sign of a few very large values, just like the billionaire in the room. Half the town earns less than $42k, not $95k.

📘 POLI-LIT Codebook

Teaching dataset for this course. Unit of analysis: country-year (each row = one country in one year). Years: 1990–2014. Sources: Varieties of Democracy (V-Dem) v15 and the Correlates of War (COW) Intergovernmental Organizations Dataset v3. Use: teaching and learning only; not for research or reports in other courses.

Variables

Variable Type Description
year Interval Year of observation (1990–2014).
country_name Nominal Country name.
country_id Nominal Numeric ID from V-Dem, used to link datasets.
v2x_polyarchy Interval Electoral Democracy Index (0–1). Built from suffrage, clean elections, elected officials, freedom of association, and freedom of expression. Higher = more democratic. Source: V-Dem.
e_gdppc Ratio GDP per capita. Treat units as thousands (1 = 1,000 U.S. dollars). Source: V-Dem.
e_regionpol_6C Nominal Region code: 1 = Eastern Europe and Central Asia, 2 = Latin America and the Caribbean, 3 = Middle East and North Africa, 4 = Sub-Saharan Africa, 5 = Western Europe and North America, 6 = Asia and Pacific.
demregion Interval Regional democracy average (0–1): the mean v2x_polyarchy of all other countries in the same region. Constructed for teaching.
v2smgovsmcenprc Interval How much the government censors political content on social media. Higher values = less censorship. Expert-coded. Source: V-Dem.
v2x_gender Interval Gender Equality Index (0–1). Higher = greater equality. Source: V-Dem.
IGO_count Ratio Number of intergovernmental organization (IGO) memberships. Source: COW.
IGO_participation_level Ordinal Categories made from IGO_count: Low (≤50), Medium (51–70), High (≥71). Constructed for teaching.

Things to keep in mind

  • Expert judgments can vary. Scores may shift from year to year because of how they’re estimated, not only because of real change.
  • Indexes are built from many pieces, and each index has strengths and weaknesses.
  • IGO counts treat every organization the same: the UN counts the same as a small regional body.
  • Coverage isn’t perfect. Some countries or years are missing (NA).
