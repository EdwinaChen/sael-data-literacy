Subunit 4: Data Ethics

About 10 minutes · 3 short sections

You’ve learned how data are made, organized, and shown. This last subunit asks a different question: what do we owe the people behind the numbers, and the people who will read them? We look at it from two sides:

  1. When you’re shown data: in the news, on social media, in a report.
  2. When you use data: in your Issue Report, a presentation, or your own research someday.

As before, quick questions unlock the next section. They are not graded.

Section 4.1

When you’re shown data

Data can feel objective, but data is never neutral. Every dataset reflects choices about who collected it, what was counted, and how it’s presented. Before you accept a number, ask:

  • Who collected it, and why? Who benefits if you believe it? A government might highlight falling unemployment and leave out flat wages.
  • What’s missing? Which years, places, or people aren’t covered? A survey that skips rural areas tells a city story.
  • Whose voices are left out? People without internet access, or who don’t answer surveys, can disappear from the data.
  • What could go wrong if it’s misused? Numbers that seem harmless can still hurt people, as the game below shows.

“Anonymous” isn’t always anonymous

Removing names doesn’t always protect people. A few ordinary details, combined, can point to one person. A famous 2000 study estimated that 87% of Americans could be uniquely identified by just their ZIP code, gender, and full birth date. A 2006 re-check with newer census data put it at 63%: lower, but still most people. (Sweeney 2000; Golle 2006)

Section 4.2

When you use data

In this course you’re an advocate: you use evidence to persuade. That makes the line between persuading and misleading especially important. When you use data:

  • Respect people. If your data involve people, such as a survey of classmates, ask permission and protect their identities.
  • Be transparent. Say where your numbers come from and what choices you made, like which years or which cases.
  • Don’t distort. No cut-off bar axes, and no showing only the years or cases that support your point (Subunit 3).
  • Own your mistakes. If you find an error, correct it openly. Your credibility depends on it.

A simple test: if the people in your data, or a reader who disagrees with you, saw exactly how you made your chart, would they say it was fair?

Is it OK? Three quick scenarios

Scenario 1

For your Issue Report presentation, you start a bar chart’s axis at 50 so the problem looks more urgent.

Starting bars above zero exaggerates the differences (remember the gender-by-region chart). Persuade with the real size of the problem.

Scenario 2

You surveyed classmates for your report and plan to quote their answers with their names, without asking them.

People should know how their answers will be used. Ask first, or share answers without names, and watch for details that could identify someone.

Scenario 3

Your data cover 2010–2024, but you only show 2018–2024 because those years support your argument best.

Choosing only the years that help your case misleads readers. Show the full period, or explain clearly why you focus on certain years.

Section 4.3

Who checks? And where to learn more

Institutional Review Boards (IRBs)

Universities don’t rely on good intentions alone. At U.S. universities, an Institutional Review Board (IRB) reviews research involving people before it starts. It checks that participation is voluntary, that people give informed consent, that their information is kept confidential, and that risks are as low as possible. For example, if a researcher wanted to survey students about their voting habits, the IRB would make sure no one felt pressured and that answers stayed anonymous. At USC, this is the job of the USC IRB in the Office of Research Compliance.
